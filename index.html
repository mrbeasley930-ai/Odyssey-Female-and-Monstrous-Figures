What you are doing

Odysseus meets five female figures on the way home who could stop him getting there. Each of you takes one of them. You read her passages in Wilson's translation, answer the questions, and then make a set of judgements about her.

Everyone makes the same judgements, so your answers go into one grid that the whole class can see. When all five columns are filled in, you compare the figures and agree three conclusions about gender and danger in the poem.

Have your copy of Wilson open. The line numbers are Wilson's. If a passage seems to start a line or two early or late, read from the nearest full stop.

Choose your figure

The class grid

This fills in as each person works. It checks for new answers every fifteen seconds while this tab is open.

Compare the five

Work through these with the grid in front of you. Each person speaks for their own figure.

  1. Sort the five into those that threaten Odysseus's life and those that threaten his homecoming, which the Greeks called his nostos. Which kind does the poem spend more lines on? Which kind is harder for him to resist?
  2. Which figures speak or sing, and which have only a body or a movement? What do the ones with a voice use it for?
  3. Look along the row on how he gets past. How often is it his own cleverness, and how often is he following instructions from a god or a goddess?
  4. Look along the row on cost. Who pays each time?
  5. Two of the five end up helping him. What has to happen before a dangerous goddess becomes a helpful one?
  6. Circe and Calypso are both found singing at a loom, and so is Penelope in Ithaca. Where would Penelope go on this grid? Where would Nausicaa and Arete from Books 6 and 7 go?
  7. Four of these five are described by Odysseus himself to the Phaeacians, who have promised him a ship. Only Calypso in Book 5 is described by the poet. Does that change how far you trust each picture?

Your three conclusions

Agree three statements about gender and danger in the Odyssey that the grid supports. Under each one, name the two figures that prove it best. Anyone can type here and everyone sees the same boxes, so choose one person to type at a time.
